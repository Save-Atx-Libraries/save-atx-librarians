Chapter 03 · How Austin was drawn “rich”

The district’s map is older than the deficit.

This chapter is the city map, not the finance lawsuits. Recapture and “Robin Hood” are on the history page. The 2026 closures also sit on a city that used schools to decide who would live where. Under-enrollment on the East Side is not a one-year surprise.

1928: schools as a segregation tool

In 1927 the Austin City Council hired Koch & Fowler. The January 1928 report, A City Plan for Austin, Texas, is remembered because it recommended concentrating Black residents east of East Avenue (now I-35) by putting “all the facilities and conveniences” — including schools — only in that district.

The plan said racial zoning was legally unavailable after Buchanan v. Warley. It then described the workaround: draw services to one area so the population would follow. Quote from the plan, widely reproduced in city and press histories:

“This will eliminate the necessity of duplication of white and black schools, white and black parks, and other duplicate facilities.”

Zavala Elementary opened in 1936 for Mexican American students. Black schools outside East Austin, including Wheatsville, closed as families were pushed east. East Avenue later became the interstate. Redlining and deed restrictions did the rest. This is city planning history, not a slogan.

Sources: 1928 Austin city plan (document history) · KUT, 2019 · Austin History Center reporting via the Statesman.

What the plan did to schools

It treated “duplicate” Black schools as a cost to be avoided — by moving people, not by equalizing campuses. That logic — fewer buildings, concentrated “need,” savings on duplication — rhymes with later consolidation language. Rhyme is not identity. The documents still sit in a line.

Desegregation, one-way busing, then closures

  1. 1971
    Original L.C. Anderson High School closed

    A federal judge ordered the Black high school closed as part of a desegregation case. AISD began one-way busing of Black students to predominantly white campuses. The campus lineage goes back to Robertson Hill (1889).

  2. 1976
    Palm School closed

    Historic East Austin school that had come to serve primarily Hispanic students. Board cited facility condition.

  3. 1987
    Lawsuit over East Austin investment

    A suit accused the district of withholding enrichment programs, delaying repairs, and under-equipping minority-neighborhood schools — summarized in Austin History Center files cited by the Statesman in 2019.

  4. 2008
    Johnston High School closed and reopened

    East Austin high school closed for academic reasons and reopened as Eastside Memorial on the same site.

  5. 2019
    Brooke, Metz, Sims, Pease

    Board closed four elementaries; three were East Austin campuses. Officials cited chronic under-enrollment. Community testimony at the time warned about repeating historical practice. Metz had been built small and close to other East Side schools — a segregated-era footprint.

  6. 2025–26
    Eleven campuses, including International High School

    Board-approved consolidations for 2026–27: Barrington, Becker, Dawson, Oak Springs, Ridgetop, Sunset Valley, Widen, Winn Montessori, Bedichek, Martin, plus International High School; Paredes later. About 3,796 students reassigned; about 6,319 seats removed. Savings estimated ~$21.5 million, with ~$17 million already spoken for by turnaround plans.

Closure lists: AISD consolidations · KXAN FAQ · Statesman closure timeline.

Why under-enrollment is not a sudden mystery

East Austin schools were often built smaller and closer together because they served legally separate student bodies. After white flight and later gentrification, family counts in those attendance zones fell. New residents in some central tracts do not have school-age children at the same rate. Charter enrollment pulled additional students who still live inside AISD’s boundary — the Statesman reported about 16,500 such students in 2026 coverage of the deficit.

Texas funds on average daily attendance, not enrollment. Empty seats still have buildings, utilities, and staffing formulas. Closing a campus can be a rational budget move in a single year. It does not unwind the 1928 decision to stack Black and Mexican American schools on one side of a highway, then starve or bus them later.

The 2026 property strategy treats some of those sites as surplus: estimated one-time value around $15 million each for a set of campuses, with ~$60 million written into deficit math. One-time land money is a bandage on a recurring operating wound. Land sold is not a librarian, a bus, or an inflation-indexed allotment.

The patch on this chapter

Consolidate, reassign ~3,800 students, sell or lease four properties, and say the remaining campuses will be better resourced. That can be true on a spreadsheet. It does not change the city’s racial geography or the state’s wealth-per-student test that still calls AISD rich.
