After the record

A city is neighborhoods. The process treated them as empty seats.

Austin is a big city with its own cultures, block by block. West Austin can often raise PTA money and fill volunteer rolls that East and North Austin cannot. That is not because some children deserve more. It is the setup. Closing a school, moving a bus stop, or running a one-minute comment clock is a decision about whether those neighborhoods still get to shape their own future. In 2025–26 the district and the state made that harder, not easier.

What still needs doing

The numbers are already on this site. The unfinished work is whether people who live here can still take part in the decisions that change their actual community.

How the process shut people out

Local school meetings — the campus nights in the gym or cafeteria — collected testimony while trustees sat silent. Public comment ran on a one-minute clock the board is told not to answer. The consolidation vote came after the plan had been on the calendar for months. Last-round savings from 2019 were never published as a condition of another yes. West and central campuses could fill the clock because they could organize. That is adult time and money, not a test of whose kids belong in a public school.

What a neighborhood school actually is

It is not an occupancy rate. It is the adults children know, the language they hear at home, the library, the walk or the bus, the PTA that can or cannot write a check. Big-city districts inherit that mix. A formula that calls Austin “property-wealthy” while nearly half of students are economically disadvantaged does not erase it. Treating the city as one spreadsheet does.

People sat through those meetings and watched children’s schools get sealed from AISD’s central offices, after the neighborhood no longer had a real say. That is not participation. That is being told when it is already too late. The ask of the district, and of the state, is not mysterious: understand that a big city has its own cultures, block by block, and stop designing a year in which the only public act left is a one-minute card, a campus meeting with no questions, and a vote that was going to happen anyway.

Who can still change what

AISD Board of Trustees

Can still choose where cuts land, whether trustees take questions at the school meetings they were elected to hear, whether last-round savings are published before another closure vote, whether bus hubs are modeled against attendance, and whether a neighborhood school is treated as more than a seat count.

Texas Legislature / TEA

Owns the basic allotment, recapture, and the A–F clock that turned empty seats into a reason to close buildings. A $55 add-on does not keep up with inflation. The rainy-day fund held $27.5 billion on June 30, 2026 and has already hit its cap. That pile is an investment the state is not making in classrooms, water, and the grid — not a one-time gift to “solve” a crisis. A takeover threat is not a substitute for funding the school day in a large city whose neighborhoods are not interchangeable.

A comment that names the community

1. Say where you live and what the school still does for that neighborhood.

2. Name the process problem (one-minute clock, no questions at the campus night, a vote after the plan was already set).

3. Ask the board — and, if it is a formula question, the Legislature — to treat Austin’s neighborhoods as places with people in them, not as empty seats on a slide.
