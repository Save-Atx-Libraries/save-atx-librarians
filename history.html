02 · Recapture is a court order

Recapture is a court settlement. “Robin Hood” is a newspaper name for it.

Austin ISD did not invent the rule that sends local property tax out of the district. Texas courts struck down the old system for leaving poor districts unable to raise what wealthy districts raised at the same tax rate. The Legislature’s surviving answer was recapture. The press called it Robin Hood. The nickname stuck. The legal meaning of “rich” did not mean Austin families.

The sequence, without the seminar

Each row is a binding step. Skip a row and the present-day recapture slide does not make sense.

When What the record actually is Why it still matters here
1973 San Antonio ISD v. Rodriguez, U.S. Supreme Court. Education is not a federal fundamental right under the 14th Amendment. Texas’s property-tax system survived federal challenge. The fight moved to the Texas Constitution. That is why later cases are Edgewood, not another trip to Washington.
1984–89 Edgewood ISD v. Kirby (Edgewood I). Mexican American Legal Defense and Educational Fund files for Edgewood, a property-poor San Antonio district. Texas Supreme Court, unanimous, Oct. 2, 1989: the system violates Article VII, Section 1 — the duty to provide an “efficient system of public free schools.” Holding: districts must have “substantially equal access to similar revenues per pupil at similar levels of tax effort.” The court also wrote that a “Band-Aid will not suffice.” The problem the court named was unequal access to revenue at a given tax rate — not Austin’s 2026 librarian line. Recapture is one later attempt to answer that order.
1990–92 Edgewood II (1991): Senate Bill 1 still unconstitutional. Edgewood III (1992): “County Education Districts” struck down as an unlawful statewide property tax. Voters then reject a constitutional amendment that would have authorized that tax. A statewide school property tax is hard to do in Texas. Sharing local surplus became the path that was left.
1993 Senate Bill 7 (May 28). Property-wealthy districts must reduce revenue above a wealth cap. Five options: merge tax bases, send money to the state, contract to educate students elsewhere, consolidate, or transfer commercial property. Sending money to the state is what most large districts do. That payment is recapture (now Chapter 49; it was Chapter 41). This is the statute AISD still lives under. It was written to answer Edgewood, not to label households on the East Side “rich.”
Same years Newspapers nickname the scheme Robin Hood: take from “rich” districts, give to “poor” ones. The Texas Handbook and later statute primers still record that the name came from the press, not from the bill title. The brand is moral shorthand. The legal test is taxable property value per student. Austin can be “Robin Hood rich” and 48.4% economically disadvantaged at the same time. Both can be true because they measure different things.
1995 Edgewood IV (Edgewood ISD v. Meno): Texas Supreme Court upholds Senate Bill 7. Recapture becomes the settlement that survived. Changing recapture is a legislative (or constitutional) job. A local cut list cannot repeal Edgewood IV.
2019 House Bill 3 raises the basic allotment to $6,160, compresses tax rates, and recodifies recapture as Chapter 49. Last real lift of the base dollar. See inflation for what happened when that number did not move with prices.
2025–26 House Bill 2 adds $55 (GYIA) for the biennium. AISD adopts a balanced 2026–27 budget after an $181 million projected hole, with $715.5 million adopted recapture and campus-level reductions. The 1993 tool is still running. The 2019 dollar was not indexed. What a school board can still vote — closures, class size, buses — is the remainder.

Case cites: Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 1 (1973); Edgewood I, 777 S.W.2d 391 (Tex. 1989); Edgewood IV, 917 S.W.2d 717 (Tex. 1995). Nickname: Texas State Historical Association, “Edgewood ISD v. Kirby”; recapture codified at Education Code Chapter 49. Austin map (1928 plan, busing, closures) is a separate chapter: austin.html.

What Edgewood was actually about

Edgewood Independent School District sat in Bexar County with a small tax base. Alamo Heights, in the same county, sat on a much larger one. The 1989 opinion used statewide extremes as well: on the order of a 700-to-1 gap in property wealth per student between the richest and poorest districts. Poor districts could tax high and still not match what wealthy districts raised while taxing low.

The Texas Constitution does not say “equal spending in every classroom.” It says the Legislature shall provide an efficient system of public free schools. The court read “efficient” to require a close correlation between tax effort and the resources a child actually gets. That is the sentence later statutes are trying to satisfy.

The Legislature has no state income tax to lean on. School money is mostly local property tax plus a state formula. After voters refused a constitutional patch for county-wide school taxes, Senate Bill 7 kept local districts and skimmed “excess” local revenue from those over a wealth threshold. Option 3 — write a check to the state — is recapture as Austin meets it.

The nickname vs. the statute

Robin Hood (press): steal from the rich, give to the poor.

Recapture (law): a Chapter 49 district’s local M&O collections above its formula entitlement are not kept. “Wealth” is property value per weighted student, not a family’s W-2.

If the nickname is the only story you bring to a board meeting, the slide that says “property wealthy” will look like a moral insult. If you bring the Edgewood holding, the question changes: does this 1993 tool still do what the court asked — equal access at similar tax effort — or does it now export Austin’s levy while the base dollar lags inflation?

If the point is to change something

History is only useful here if it names who can still move which piece.

Cannot be voted away on June 18

Article VII, Section 1. Edgewood I’s equal-access holding. Edgewood IV’s approval of SB 7. Chapter 49 recapture. ADA-based state aid. The statutory basic allotment of $6,160 (plus whatever GYIA the Legislature sets).

Can still be chosen locally

Where cuts land (campus vs central). Whether August “replacement” money for librarians comes off another student-facing line. Bus hubs vs modeled ADA loss. Whether closed land is sold as one-time cash. Whether the board’s public packet puts recapture and the inflation gap on the same page as class size.

The 1989 court said a bandage would not suffice for the system. Campus cuts in 2026 can still be a bandage on that same system. That is a description of jurisdiction — state formula vs local remainder — not a vibe. Full Austin geography (why East Side campuses were small and close together) is the next chapter.
