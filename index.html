Public-record briefing · updated August 2026

Every Austin student deserves good teachers, a full school day, and a funded classroom.

Austin ISD did not invent Texas school finance. Recapture, a frozen state allotment, and a real operating shortfall still do not make it sound policy to cut educators, close neighborhood schools, and treat children as occupancy rates. Students in every zip code deserve good teachers and a funded school day.

This city

Income areas in Austin are different. That should not be a ranking of children. For years it has been one.

Some neighborhoods can still raise PTA money and fill volunteer rolls during the school day. Others cannot. That is a fact about adult time and money, not about whose kids belong in a public school. West Austin children did not invent the surplus. East and North Austin children are not a hole in a spreadsheet. A public system is supposed to put a floor under that difference. Instead, for a long stretch of this city’s school history, the difference has done the ranking: who still has a full day, who can be heard before a vote, who can buy back what the formula dropped. When the public system thins, that surplus becomes the school day — two different days inside one district.

In 2025–26, showing up was often still not a say. AISD held local meetings at the schools themselves — usually in the gym or cafeteria — where families came to speak about their campus. Trustees, when they came, were often introduced and did not take questions. The vote sat on a downtown calendar. People who sat through those nights could watch a child’s school get decided after the neighborhood no longer had a real part in it.

AISD’s nine trustees are elected, not hired by the superintendent. Seven represent geographic districts; two are elected citywide. Terms are four years. They are unpaid. A vacant seat can be filled by appointment until the next election. The job, in law, is to hire and evaluate the superintendent, adopt the budget, set the tax rate, and set policy — including closures. That is real work, and it is not teaching or cleaning a hall. It is also not a party label on the ballot. Who has time to run, and who gets heard before the vote, still tracks adult time and money in this city. In this cycle the record is the vote, not a veto from the gym. See the recapture bill and what the meetings show.

Four documented facts

What the record shows, in four figures.

$715.5M FY2025–26 adopted recapture — local tax dollars AISD must send to the state under Chapter 49. The recapture bill
$181M Projected 2026–27 operating shortfall the board had to drive to zero. That is the year people called a crisis. The 2026 “crisis”
$6,160 Statutory basic allotment, set in 2019. HB 2 added $55. Inflation-adjusted 2025 value would have been about $7,756. The allotment froze
48.4% AISD students classified economically disadvantaged, inside a district the state labels property-wealthy. How Austin was drawn

Recapture is local property tax AISD must send to the state. The allotment is the state’s base dollar per student. Closures, staffing, bus hubs, and which campuses lose planning time are local choices inside that formula. Children in every zip code deserve the same public school day. What these words mean. The rainy-day fund

Texas is sitting on about $30 billion. That is not a gift. It is an investment the state is not making.

$27.5B Rainy-day fund as of June 30, 2026. Already past the constitutional cap. That is the pile still sitting there. Surplus and the rainy-day fund
$28.5B Where the Comptroller projects that same fund by the end of fiscal 2027 if the Legislature takes nothing more out. Comptroller CRE / BRE
$55 What the state added to the basic allotment this biennium. Inflation since 2019 is more than a thousand dollars per student. The allotment froze
$715.5M What Austin ISD must send the state this year in recapture — while the state’s own piggy bank sits full. The recapture bill

A rainy-day fund is supposed to wait for a downturn. Texas has filled this one past the cap the constitution set — about $30 billion, in round numbers — and it is still parking it. Spending that money on classrooms, school buildings, water, and the power grid is not a one-time bailout. Children are not a flood. They come back every year. Putting the surplus into those things is an investment in the state’s own infrastructure and in the people who will still live here.

The $181 million hole AISD closed on campuses is a rounding error next to that pile. Texas is holding tens of billions while classrooms lose librarians, bus stops, and planning time. Rainy-day fund figures →

The briefing

How the 2026 cuts were produced.

Recapture was built to answer a court order. Austin was labeled property-wealthy while nearly half of its students are economically disadvantaged. The basic allotment froze. The 2026 budget closed a shortfall on campuses. A public school system cannot treat PTA fundraising as a substitute for teachers, librarians, and buses.
