What each night actually contained

The caption folder has fifteen files: regular voting meetings, information sessions, budget work sessions, two public hearings, and one short budget update. This page is the nights that can be described from those tracks and from the write-ups published afterward. A vote count below is the chair’s spoken count unless the sentence says it comes from coverage.

Voting nights open with one-minute comments, in the room or by a phone message signed up in advance. Under the open-meetings law the board does not answer them. The votes come later, often after 10 p.m. and sometimes after midnight, and they are hands. If every person seated votes yes, the record is “all those on the dais,” which hides an absence. Work sessions do not take those votes. The hearings on February 26 and June 18 adjourn into the voting meeting. The campus-closure votes of November 20–21, 2025 are not in the folder.

December 18, 2025 Regular voting meeting

Consent agenda. Motion Kathryn Whitley Chu, second Candace Hunter. The chair’s count was all those on the dais. No budget adoption this night. The November closures were already behind them, and they are not on this tape.

February 26, 2026 Public hearing, then a voting meeting

The hearing adjourns into the voting meeting. This page does not have a comment-by-comment reading of that hearing. It is in the set so the June 18 hearing is not treated as the only one.

March 26, 2026 Regular voting meeting

Consent: motion Kevin Foster, second Arati Singh, all on the dais.

Item 12.1, Austin Community College dual-credit agreements: Foster, second Andrew Gonzales. 8–0–1. Hunter abstained. That abstention is one of the few names the chair actually says in this whole stack.

Items 13.1 and 13.2, two contract terminations: Gonzales, second Hunter, all on the dais, each item.

April 7, 2026 Budget work session. No vote.

The district called it to order as a work session on the 2026–27 budget. The number in the room was a $181 million hole for the coming year. Community Impact, writing that night, said staff walked four scenarios of $117 million to $132 million in cuts, that Chief Financial Officer Katrina Montgomery put another $9 million to $10 million on vacant positions and $50 million on monetizing three properties, and that the package could take the hole down to about $4 million or a small surplus. The next morning the superintendent’s own letter said the current year was now expected to end $49 million in the red, $30 million worse than the summer projection, and listed what “deeper reductions” meant: campus staffing and master schedules, planning periods, ratios for campus administrators and support staff, stipends, and 15 percent off non-staffing budgets. The November closures, he wrote, were already counted at about $21 million. Preliminary budget on April 23. Final vote June 18.

On the caption, a trustee said the instinct would be no, don’t cut that, and then said the district was no longer at a point where it could protect all things. After the session, Segura told reporters the district had kept earlier cuts off the classroom “and it had limited impacts to our students,” and then: “But we’re at a point now … where we’re no longer able to protect those cuts from impacting our classrooms.” That is the staff sentence. It is not a sentence anybody at the microphone asked for.

April 23, 2026 Regular voting meeting. Preliminary budget is on the calendar.

The public comment that the captions actually name is a string of speech-language pathologists. Shannon Donahue. Tracy Easley Carr at LBJ and Sadler Means. Sheila Ray at Allison. Zeb White and Kelly Keenan at Doss. Anne Jefferson at Highland Park. Same point from each of them. The district had just come off a TEA special-education order because those jobs stayed filled. A stipend cut is less pay for the same job, not a cut that also cuts duty days. They named 2020, when evaluation staff left and the backlog followed. A parent the same month said a work session had floated about $2 million by converting librarian roles, and asked the board to find it somewhere else.

The vote did not touch any of that as its own item. Consent, including items 9.1 through 9.8, 10.1 through 10.3, 11.1 through 11.4, 12.1, 13.1, and 13.2: all on the dais. Items 14.1 and 14.3, employee terminations: Foster, second David Kauffman, all on the dais, each.

April 30, 2026 Budget work session. Called to order 6:10 p.m. No vote.

President Lynn Boswell opened it. Staff put the same $181 million gross deficit on three fund-balance targets, with $50 million of property monetization sitting beside the cuts. Spoken in the district’s posted caption: to land at 15 percent in reserve, reduce $127 million; at 16 percent, $135 million; at 17 percent, $143 million. The $50 million is on top of those figures, not inside them.

Then staff read the public’s list back into the record. Protect class size and classroom staffing. Preserve planning minutes. Keep nurses, counselors, librarians, and assistants. Protect health benefits and the bilingual and special-education stipends. Keep transportation for high-need students. Look at central office before campuses. Scheduling, they said, should follow that first priority, which was planning time. The June 18 budget does not follow this list. Both nights are on the record.

May 7, 2026 Information session. The Chronicle’s account of the room.

The Austin Chronicle reported the seats filled that evening. Education Austin’s signs asked for three things: protect campus-based support staff, keep special-education and bilingual stipends, and keep a 6-of-8 secondary schedule. The proposal on the table was the opposite shape. Non-core middle and high school teachers would lose one of two planning periods and teach seven periods out of eight. The following year, the reduced planning time would reach the rest of secondary teachers, except elementary and except core teachers at campuses with unacceptable ratings. On the district’s own posted discussion of that plan, staff described it as a two-year phase and, at one point, as landing near “six and a half of eight” once the bands were averaged. A theater teacher told them a non-core label meant seven of eight next year, five preps against one or two, and special-education students in those classes with less time to plan.

Segura’s frame, as the Chronicle reported it, was the recapture check: more than $715.5 million in 2026, just under half of a $1.7 billion adopted budget. The 2022 bond, he said, cannot be spent to balance this budget. Miss a balanced budget by June 30 and the state can take the district over. Recommended budget May 21. Adoption June 18.

May 21, 2026 Regular voting meeting. Called to order 6:04 p.m. Ran past 1 a.m.

Item 10.6 was a financial report. It was not a standalone vote on the 7-of-8 schedule. People used it as the hook anyway.

Aaron Boojhawon, choir at Bowie — the auto-caption hears the name as Aaron Bajois — said fine-arts colleagues were already being told they would not return. He told the board the savings were a small fraction of the deficit and that seven of eight is how a program erodes: feeder programs cut back, teachers pushed outside their concentration, the next crisis built in advance. Other speakers on the same item: a 20-year art teacher, Doss speech pathologists, a CTE teacher, Walnut Creek callers, substitutes. Planning time, stipend cuts, special education. One speaker’s ask was blunt: do not approve seven of eight, and do not put extra students in the classes.

Nobody pulled 10.6. Consent covered items 8.1 through 8.5, 9.1 through 9.8, 10.1 through 10.6, 11.1, and 12.1 through 12.20. Motion Foster, second LaRessa Quintana. Fernando Lucas de Urioste was in by video. The chair’s count was all those on the dais. The schedule change, the stipend question, and the financial report passed inside that block.

Later, item 14.1, a proposed termination. Foster, second Hunter. The chair first said four. Around 1 a.m. the count was corrected: 5 yes, 0 no, Quintana abstaining.

June 4, 2026 Board meeting, from the Chronicle’s June 11 account. Not a vote night.

Librarians were in the room. The Chronicle photographed the protest and the board taking comment on the recommended budget. Gabby Garcia, associate librarian at McBee Elementary, told trustees she could not afford the part-time job being offered. Alexia Haywood, bilingual librarian at Uphaus Early Childhood Center, had received a letter from her principal on May 28, the students’ last day, that she might not have a job in the fall. The proposal the Chronicle described: 23 smaller schools at half a librarian, to save $977,500. A speaker’s warning, printed without a clean name attached, was that when the part-time librarian is gone the doors are shut and the lights are off. The same article said some administrative jobs would lose days and pay, students would lose one-to-one devices, and the district hoped to raise $60 million by selling or leasing four campuses already set to close. Candace Hunter, the Chronicle wrote, was already unsure she could vote for that version. The calendar still said June 18, and still said a missed deadline could mean a state takeover.

June 11, 2026 Comment on the record, and a rally outside

On the caption, a speaker said the superintendent had gone back on a promise not to touch librarians. That is the speaker’s claim, not a finding. The Statesman photographed a rally against the cuts outside district headquarters the same day: transportation, positions, benefits, the $181 million hole. The Chronicle’s June 11 piece is mostly the June 4 meeting, not a second transcript. The two should not be folded together.

June 18, 2026 Public hearing, then the voting meeting. Adjourned 12:42 a.m.

The Chronicle timed it at almost seven hours, ending close to midnight. The hearing is the public half. The voting meeting is where the hands go up. The Statesman put President Boswell, Vice President Gonzales, Secretary Foster, and Superintendent Matias Segura at the table for the comment period, and photographed people in the room in black, which the paper described as mourning the jobs.

What people spent the minute on

Fine arts, by name. Lamar parents Dan Schmidt, Whitney, and Meredith said five fine-arts positions were on the list, including an assistant band director and a theater director, and that choir at O. Henry was already closed. The Statesman said the asks also included guitar classes, planning time, mentorship contracts, and after-school buses. No separate arts item was on the agenda. Those jobs lived or died inside the budget.

Librarians, by count. Speakers said 16 of the 23 campuses losing librarian time were Title I, and that some librarians had already been offered a split week before the vote. The proposal in the paper was 11.5 positions and a half-time librarian at 23 schools, to save nearly $1 million. The Chronicle’s figure for that savings is $977,500.

The rest of the room, from coverage rather than from a caption line: Eric Ramos, a teacher and treasurer of Education Austin, spoke about what would happen to a campus already set to close. Jessica Meza, math at Northeast Early College High School, said of the campus cuts, “I think we should have put up a bigger fight.”

The contract, before the fight

Item 14.1, the superintendent’s evaluation and contract amendment. Foster, second Hunter. No opposition. FOX 7 reported the extension through August 2030 at $362,250 a year. The same story said a $1,500 payment for each campus that moved a D or an F up to at least a C had been in the posted draft and was not in the document they voted. On the tape, Segura said the paper in front of them was not the one uploaded earlier. The salary and the deleted bonus are from that coverage, not from a dollar figure read in the vote sentence. The cut list and the contract are the same agenda. The contract was the easy vote.

Three tries at the librarians

The caption reading used on this site, and the next-day stories, do not name the movers the same way. The counts mostly agree. The names do not. Both are printed here. The tape’s own chair does not call the roll.

First try, as this project read the caption: Arati Singh. Eliminate the deputy superintendent’s office and the ombudsman office, and use that money for a full-time librarian on every campus. Failed. 2 in favor, 5 opposed, 2 abstentions. FOX 7 describes that same count — 5 to 2, two abstaining — and does not name the maker. Community Impact names a different trustee, Kathryn Whitley Chu, on an amendment to drop the deputy superintendent, and says the funding came up about $477,000 short. Those can be the same motion misheard, or two motions. This page does not pick.

Second try, on the caption: Candace Hunter, second Quintana. Use only the deputy superintendent’s salary, and cover as many schools as that salary would buy. Segura said it would not cover them. The smallest schools, the ones already at the end of an enrollment list, would be the ones left out. Failed. 3 in favor, 5 opposed, 1 abstention. FOX 7’s sequence is close and not identical: a trustee asked Segura to find $1.1 million, withdrew that wording after discussion, and came back with “use the deputy superintendent’s pay for as many librarians as it buys.” The paper says that reworded version failed, and it uses “she.” Hunter is the “she” that fits. The caption does not include a withdrawal.

What passed, 6–3, was the fund-balance version. On the tape Segura described it as about a tenth of a percent off the reserve target, from about 13.1 percent to 13.01 percent, and said he would bring a real offset back in August. Figures tossed on that tape were about $900,000, and a salary that did not cover every campus. The chair did not say who made the motion. The Chronicle and Community Impact both name David Kauffman, minutes before the vote. Community Impact adds a second from Andrew Gonzales, and both papers put the draw at $1.1 million, or “close to $1 million.” The district’s June 19 release says nearly $1 million, from fund balance, to be restored in August with cuts Segura had not yet named. Secretary Foster had said, earlier that night, that he wanted to stop adding amendments and vote the budget as presented, because each new change created pain they would not understand until morning. The fund-balance change happened anyway.

Coverage of the argument, not of the arithmetic: trustees asked why librarians rather than some other cut, and whether a reserve already projected to finish the year near 10 percent — half the old 20 percent policy, under the 15 percent floor the board had temporarily allowed — should be the place the $1 million came from.

The budget those tries sat inside

Item 14.2, as amended. General fund, food service, debt service. Foster, second Singh. 7 yes, 1 no, 1 abstention. The tape does not say who the one and the abstention were. Community Impact and other next-day coverage name Quintana as the no and leave the abstention unnamed. The district’s release the next morning: an $887 million budget after $205 million in operating reductions, more than 500 positions, a $19 million addition to savings, and $20 million of further cuts so the reserve might reach 13 percent by the end of the new year from a projected 10 percent. FOX 7 and the Chronicle say 558 positions, some vacant. KXAN’s figure for the reductions was nearly $186 million. The district’s number is the one this site uses, and the others are why the headlines do not match.

What that package still did, because the librarian amendment did not touch it. Class sizes up. Planning time down — the Chronicle’s phrase was that some teachers’ planning time was cut in half. Bilingual and special-education stipends cut, which is the opposite of the April 30 list and the opposite of what the speech pathologists asked for on April 23. Neighborhood middle and high school stops replaced with hubs; the Chronicle put that at 46.5 bus-driver positions. One-to-one devices ended. The district counted on about $60 million from property sales before the fiscal year ended. The Statesman’s cut list also included fewer elementary fine-arts options and smaller social-work contracts. Communities in Schools, the Chronicle wrote, lost funding at some campuses. None of those had their own roll call.

The district’s June 19 release also said the adopted budget carried $1.48 billion in local tax revenue and $630 million to the state as recapture. That is the district’s figure for the budget voted that night. It is not the $715.5 million adopted-recapture line for 2025–26, and the two should not be added or swapped.

Items 16.1 and 16.2, contract terminations, all on the dais. Adjourned 12:42 a.m. The comments had been at the start.

What is left after the nights are written out

  • The public’s list is readable. April 23 is the stipends. April 30 is the staff reading that list back. May 7 and May 21 are the schedule. June 4 and June 18 are the librarians and the arts jobs. The replies are shorter than the asks.
  • April 30 and June 18 are allowed to contradict each other. They do. The stipends, the planning time, and the central-office-first order did not survive. The librarian line survived only as a draw on savings.
  • The one partial win was late, one year, and not the trade the comments kept naming. The deputy superintendent’s office was still there the next morning. The reserve was smaller.
  • Dissent is real and, on the budget, unnamed on the tape. A 7–1–1 is a count. Coverage supplied Quintana’s name the next day and did not supply the abstention. The 6–3 on librarians is also a count without names on the tape. The papers name Kauffman as the maker and still do not name the three no votes.
  • Where this page and a newspaper disagree on who moved an amendment, both sentences stay. The chair did not resolve it.
