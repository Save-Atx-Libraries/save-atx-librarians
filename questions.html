09 · What the meetings show

The district built a year around TEA’s clock. Students live on a different one.

Austin ISD spent fall 2025 treating Texas Education Agency (TEA) takeover risk and empty-seat counts as the master problem, then spent spring 2026 cutting teachers’ time, buses, and librarians. Recapture is real. The frozen allotment is real. Campuses with consecutive F ratings are real. None of that required one-minute public comment, silent trustees in gyms, or a June list that took planning time, buses, stipends, and librarians after a year of promising the opposite. Students deserve the adults who make a school work. They are not occupancy rates on a slide. What TAP, TEA, and the other shorthand mean →

Where these quotes come from

The meetings were recorded. This page is what was said.

AISD videos its board meetings and campus nights. Those videos have automatic captions — the on-screen subtitles, saved as a transcript of who said what. Quotes below from fall 2025 come from those transcripts. Spring–June 2026 also uses AISD budget pages and news coverage. Automatic subtitles sometimes scramble names. Where a named roll-call vote is not in the transcript, this page does not invent hands. A TAP is a Turnaround Plan — paperwork the TEA requires for a campus that has failed its A–F rating. Other shorthand is on What these words mean.

What the state grades

TEA letter grades (A–F). “Unacceptable” (UA) campuses in a chain of failing years. Turnaround Plans the TEA will accept. How full buildings are (staff aimed at 85% from 76%). Recapture as a cash date. A November 21 TEA upload. Empty seats compared to the Moody Center. Letter grades that, in Superintendent Segura’s words, introduce risk “not by us by the state of Texas.”

What a student actually gets

A known adult. Planning time. A librarian. A neighborhood bus stop, not a hub at another campus. Dual language that continues. A counselor who is not also covering other jobs. Special-education services that do not move 850 kids as a side effect. A campus that was not cut the same year it earned an F.

What that split is getting at: downtown was solving a report card. Neighborhoods were watching their school get decided without them. You can sit through a meeting in tears for kids whose fate is already on a slide, because the vote was going to happen anyway. TEA letter grades are not fake. Using them as permission to cut the school day — and calling that “values” — is a local choice. Lauren Smith, AVID coordinator at Bedichek, said on September 18 that the year the campus earned an F, “one assistant principal and one counselor position were cut.” Then the district came back with a TAP and closure.

How we got here

The district ignored the pattern, then used the budget to finish the job.

None of the 2025–26 tools were new. Enrollment had been falling for a decade. Recapture had been paid for two decades. The allotment sat at $6,160 from 2019. East Austin was stacked with small schools by a 1928 city plan. The 2019 closures left Metz and Brook empty — public said so on October 9. Consecutive campus failure was already operating language in the 2023–24 joint subcommittees. Sitting still was a decision. So was treating subtraction as the only remaining tool.

  1. 1928–2019
    The map was the first metric the district did not undo.

    The Koch & Fowler plan used schools to put Black residents east of East Avenue. One-way busing and later closures sat on that geography. 2019 closed East Austin campuses. Public in 2025 still asked what those closures saved and what they did to enrollment. Segura’s answer, October 30: 2019 numbers are “unusable” because the pandemic hit three months later. That may be true as accounting. It is also how a pattern becomes un-auditable. See Austin.

  2. 2019–24
    The allotment froze. Campus adults did not.

    HB 3 locked $6,160. Inflation ran. Every bilingual, compensatory, and special-ed weight multiplied against a shrinking real dollar. AISD still chose master schedules, class-size bands, and which “extras” (librarians, nurses) to keep. In October 2025 Segura listed those extras as proof of values: retained librarians, retained nurses, planning time as a “$20 million swing,” “we’ve decided not to increase class sizes.” Eight months later the 2026–27 package increased elementary ratios at Band 3–4 campuses, cut planning time, and put 11.5 librarian FTE on a cut list. The formula did not write that list. See the freeze.

  3. 2023–24
    Takeover language was already the operating system.

    Joint subcommittee meetings (April 2023, April 2024) already speak consecutive campus failure, conservatorship “one step below a takeover,” and a special-education audit path. Burnet, Webb, and Dobie are, by November 20, 2025, in year five of the UA chain — the year the commissioner shall close or install a board of managers. The district had years. What it did with them is TAP paperwork and, in Smith’s account, cutting the AP and counselor at a campus that then failed.

  4. Fall 2025
    One tool. Many meetings. Same ending.

    Closures and TAP dominate every long board night and every campus gym. Staff aim at 85% utilization from 76%. Empty seats are “3,000 more than the Moody Center.” Public asks a moratorium (September 18), proof from the last round (October 30, November 20), and to decouple TAP from building closure. Segura is “adamant” against a full-year delay. November 20: TAP package yes; item 14.3 consolidations/program moves 6–3 after more than two hours of “vote no.” Recapture, inflation, and the basic allotment do not appear in that voting file.

  5. Spring–June 2026
    The values list broke on a budget slide.

    Projected shortfall $181 million. Adopted $887 million operating budget, on the order of $205 million in reductions, more than 550 positions, hubs, stipends, eleven schools already in the closure math. Librarians: promised spared, put back on the list, restored June 18 onto fund balance until August. Trustee Hunter, June 5, KUT: “When weighted it is heavier on the campus than anywhere else, the campus is carrying the brunt. And we said we weren’t gonna do that.” She named the broken promise in public. The package still landed on campuses. See the “crisis” and the cuts.

State pressure is not imaginary: Chapter 49 recapture, an unindexed allotment, A–F ratings, an agreed order, a November 21 TEA date. Local failure is not imaginary either: cutting the adults at a failing campus; treating librarians and planning time as optional until they were remainder; running 7,000 comments through a one-minute clock the board is legally told not to answer; voting yes on 14.3 after being told last-round savings “will never be forthcoming.” A district can be underfunded and still choose poorly with what remains local. AISD was both.

The public

Comment was collected. It was not taken as a constraint.

Texas law limits trustees during off-agenda comment to clarification, not debate. AISD used that rule as a wall, then collected thousands of cards, then voted the plan that had been on the calendar since February/March. Listening without changing the instrument is still a choice.

The ten questions

Asked in public. Answered from the record.

  1. Q1
    When did “this campus is failing” become “the district must cut”?

    Earlier than the 2026 deficit letter. Joint subcommittees already treat consecutive failure as a district-survival path. September 18, 2025: a Bedichek educator tells the dais the F year included cutting an AP and a counselor — an instruction problem created, then cited. October 9: Dunlap names takeover as priority one and closures as “obeying in advance.” November 20 hearing: Burnet, Webb, Dobie in year five; Segura will not ask TEA for more time for a school showing progress (“I do not believe TEA would accept it”). Campus failure is no longer treated primarily as an instruction problem. It is a document TEA will accept, due at 5 p.m. What did not arrive until 2026 was the full cut list (planning time, hubs, stipends). The legal trigger was allowed to mature for years while campus supports were already being peeled.

  2. Q2
    Why do turnaround costs eat closure savings?

    Because the year was built to feed TEA, not to net operating dollars. Staff said TAP spend happens whether or not you close; closures then become how you “afford” TAP and utilization at once. Chronicle: ~$21 million from eleven closures, ~$17 million already committed to turnaround; superintendent “almost a one to one.” Bedichek: ~$800,000 extra TAP funds at the combined campus. Hosek, November 20: about $1 million per restart/reassign campus. Closing a neighborhood school to fund a compliance plan on another campus is the instrument. Alternatives the public scored — sell empty 2019 sites first (Sparks: Metz and Brook), delay 14.3 because it is not TAP, restart Widen/Oak Springs instead of close, one-year pause — were not the motion. Quintana’s restart-Widen amendment failed. De Urioste’s Oak Springs restart failed 5–4. Segura cautioned the board against a full-year delay; he would be “adamant.”

  3. Q3
    Why were families and staff told late?

    Fall 2025: the roadmap was shown to trustees in February and March; campus meetings ran October–November; Friday, October 31 refinements; TAP add-ons at receiving schools the Friday before the vote (Ridgetop speakers: TAP “until Friday, six days ago”; support about 60% to about 90% opposed). That is a calendar that keeps changing the map after the gym. Spring 2026: campus allocations and librarian-share notices near the last week of school. Enrollment had been sliding on AISD’s own recapture table for a decade. The allotment had been frozen since 2019. A May surprise on a ten-year slide is not “the state numbers just landed.” It is waiting until the balanced-budget deadline made dissent expensive. The plan is chosen; the community is notified inside the window where urgency beats listening.

  4. Q4
    Why is “shuffle teachers / grow class size” the default labor plan?

    Because 87% of the budget is people, and the A–F score does not grade planning time. Segura, Bryker Woods: small campuses cannot fund full-time PE and fine arts without PTA subsidy; consolidation cuts a principal and admin. 2026: 112 certified staff moved; secondary planning time converted into positions; Band 3–4 ratios up. Who on the dais said larger classes at already-stressed campuses would raise achievement? Nobody in these meeting transcripts offered a learning theory. Kaufman came closest to the opposite: closures “impact outcomes in really negative ways and in the biggest negative ways for our students who need the most.” Quintana asked class-size questions at Rodriguez in the TAP hearing. Hunter talked parent conferences and campus capacity (“making phone calls at night on your way home”). Then the labor plan was still reassignment and larger ratios. The agency score wants fewer buildings and TAP staffing rules. The student gets a teacher with more children and less time, asked to ready kids for a polarized country. Those are not the same job.

  5. Q5
    Why did public comment have to rescue librarians twice?

    Because librarians were never a structural commitment. They were a brand. Fall 2025: retained, protected, loved, “above and beyond what the state requires” — already framed as optional in the same sentence as the promise. Maplewood in 2025: trading a librarian for a coach was already thinkable. April 2026: community fills the room; district had said they would be spared. June: 11.5 FTE back on the list. June 18: amendment restores them onto fund balance. That is not the public “winning librarians.” That is the administration failing twice on a line smaller than one percent of the deficit, then using reserves as a third try. If August replacement cuts come off other campus lines, students lose somewhere else.

  6. Q6
    Why do buses and attendance get treated as separate from “enrollment crisis”?

    Because utilization counts empty seats, not the ride that fills them. Texas pays on ADA. Trustee Singh named hubs as a direct hit on attendance and enrollment. The $5.1 million hub savings in the 2026 package is scored. Lost ADA is not, in the public materials cited here. October 9 already told grandfathered and new non-zone families: two-mile rule, no bus. Circle C and Zilker speakers described triple travel time. Bedichek: hazardous-intersection exceptions; “vast majority” get it — which is not a model of ADA loss. If the district tells the state it has an enrollment crisis and then makes the secondary ride a hub, it is solving a spreadsheet in a way that can worsen the formula it is paid on. That is incomplete on AISD’s own funding rule.

  7. Q7
    Why is Austin’s map treated as background rather than cause?

    Because “efficiency” is easier to say than “we inherited a segregation footprint and we are still closing the small schools it produced.” Cheryl Bradley, November 20: the district “has slid into segregationism… It’s racist. It’s classism.” Texas Appleseed the same night: dissimilarity 60%+ Black–white, Hispanic–white, and economic disadvantage; attendance zones “don’t make any sense.” East Austin speakers (Campbell as the predominantly Black elementary; Oak Springs to Blackshear; Winn to a D). West and central speakers (Bryker Woods, Becker, Ridgetop) filled the clock because those communities could organize — more volunteers, more parent time, more PTA capacity. That is a fact about adult resources, not about whose children deserve a school. Trustee Foster named “privilege protects privilege,” then voted yes. Under-enrollment on the East Side was predicted by the 1928 stack. Selling or leasing closed land as one-time cash treats a century-old pattern as surplus. See the map.

  8. Q8
    Why is inflation someone else’s job while class size is a principal’s job?

    Inflation catch-up as a number appears cleanly once in these meetings: Bedichek, staff, $1,100–$1,300 versus about $200. Recapture as a dollar appears on the dais (October 9, October 30, about $700–715 million) and at Bedichek ($714 million plus $56 million). November 20 vote night: recapture, inflation, and the allotment do not appear. Vacancy savings, TAP dollars, utilization, and TEA due dates do. Segura told Dylan the Legislature will not move recapture; the 89th session netted about $10 million; “it’s not going to change.” That can be a true political forecast. It is also how a board avoids sending a single, repeated, numbered ask — a statutory allotment indexed to inflation — and instead sends a new internal cut list. Class size becomes a principal’s remaining variable. Inflation stays a gym answer to a child.

  9. Q9
    Why are the superintendent contract and the property sales not in the same packet as class size?

    Because the packet is built to make recapture the only large number in the room. Recapture is the state’s share of local levy. It does not make local lines invisible. Public documents: superintendent base $362,250, supplemental retirement $36,750, reimbursed business travel including rental car; on the order of $60 million in one-time property monetization in deficit math. October 30: a speaker asked about a real-estate RFP that went out before the consolidation list, unnamed properties, Rosedale about $26 million; Segura said the item was a renewal of professional services, TNTP is a TAP partner not a $20 million secret contract, and disclosures have been on the website. Kaufman asked for the refutation on the record. The class-size slide still did not sit next to the travel reimbursements. Disclosure on a website is not the same as putting those lines next to 24:1 in the packet the board votes.

  10. Q10
    What is being asked of a teacher that a cut list cannot do?

    More students, less planning time, a June reassignment, TAP documentation, family-engagement phone calls on the way home, and “ready these kids” while the adults cannot keep librarians on the general fund without an amendment. Hunter described it from the kitchen table: a 50% on an assignment is not 50% of the work; light bill; packet they don’t read. Barrington teacher, ten years: third year of this superintendent, “I never seen you” except a month ago; “we are struggling emotionally.” That is the job. A one-minute comment is not a substitute for it. The common goal — a child who can read, calculate, and live with other people, including people they will disagree with, without burning out the adult — is still the test. Students in every part of Austin will share this city. The operational plan in 2025–26 did not match that. Anyone who says it did should point to the theory, on the record, with a date. These meeting transcripts do not contain that theory.

Could AISD have done otherwise?

Yes, inside the formula. Not “repeal Chapter 49 from the dais.” The local remainder was always there: where cuts land; whether last-round savings are published before another yes; whether TAP is decoupled from 14.3; whether librarians and planning time stay on the general fund or become brand; whether hubs are modeled against ADA; whether a failing campus is cut an AP and a counselor the same year it is labeled F; whether trustees take questions in the gym they were elected to hear.

Segura told Dylan the Legislature will not move recapture. Then the local job gets heavier, not lighter. What AISD did instead was obey the agency clock, collect campus testimony, and vote the buildings. That is compliance with a metric the district itself helped fail — by starving campuses of the adults who make letter grades move, then citing the letters as the reason to close the building.

The students they exist to serve are not empty-seat percentages. They are Teo asking to save his school, Rosabella asking why it is shutting down, a Barrington teacher who had not seen the superintendent, 850 special-education students in a reassignment count, and every Band 3–4 classroom that went to 24:1 after a year of “we’ve decided not to increase class sizes.” Downtown can describe a value and still cut it when the slide needs a number. Neighborhoods found out when it was already too late.

What this record does not claim

It does not claim recapture is fake, that TEA will not take districts, or that every closed building was full. It is a record of which problem the rooms were built to solve, who used a vote, and who used a speech.
